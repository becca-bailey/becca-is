Last week, I decided to look at the Epstein files for myself. I’d heard plenty of discourse about who Jeffrey Epstein associated with and what they talked about, but I wanted to understand the evidence better. So I went to [jmail.world](http://jmail.world) and started poking around.

We’ve all heard about the billionaires—Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, Bill Gates, and obviously, Donald Trump. Those names dominate headlines. But as someone who spent much of the last decade watching TED talks and following tech culture, a different set of names caught my attention.

What I found wasn’t just a list of wealthy men. It was a list of men with intellectual authority.

## The Rooms Where Legitimacy Is Made

Epstein exchanged numerous emails with and about [Richard Saul Wurman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Saul_Wurman), the architect and designer who founded TED. He also corresponded with [Kevin Slavin](https://www.media.mit.edu/people/slavin/overview/), the TED speaker known for his talk “How Algorithms Shape Our World.” These men lent him credibility and connections with other academics and thought leaders of the time.

Following that thread led me to MIT.

At MIT’s Media Lab, Epstein corresponded with [Joi Ito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joi_Ito), then director of the lab. Unlike some of the others, Ito’s relationship with Epstein is now publicly documented; he resigned from MIT in 2019 after it was revealed he had accepted significant donations from Epstein.

[Marvin Minsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_Minsky), AI pioneer and co-founder of the MIT AI Lab, was later accused by Virginia Giuffre of sexual assault. The broader circle overlapped heavily with Edge, an invitation-only “intellectual salon” that was nearly all male and deeply influential in tech’s cultural self-image.

<MarginNote>

Edge is well-known for their “billionaire dinners”. [Here’s Kara Swisher’s account from 2009](https://www.edge.org/event/the-edge-billionaires-dinner-2009).

</MarginNote>

Epstein himself had no technical expertise. What he pursued was proximity. In the 2010s, he showed particular interest in AI research, embedding himself among prominent academics, researchers, and conference organizers. Through Edge and similar networks, he placed himself inside rooms where intellectual prestige was made.

## How Much Did They Know?

So, let me get this straight.

[Epstein was first arrested for sex trafficking in 2008](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2008/jul/02/usa.internationalcrime1). That conviction was public. And yet, throughout the 2010s, he remained socially and professionally embedded in these circles. He attended events. He exchanged emails. He funded research. He had a seat at the table.

I’m not claiming that every man in these emails committed a crime. But I am saying that Epstein’s conviction was widely known, but it was not a dealbreaker.

And when you look at what was being discussed in these circles, the pattern becomes harder to ignore.

[Virginia Heffernan reported](https://www.thenerve.news/p/epstein-billionaires-eugenics-project-harvard-academia-john-brockman) on Edge’s recurring fascination with eugenics and biological hierarchy. Edge marketed itself as “a salon for the world’s finest minds.” But its roster was overwhelmingly male, and its recurring themes often centered on hierarchy—who is smarter, who is more evolved, who is naturally dominant.

So when you look at the largely white and male speaker lineups at conferences shaped by these networks, it no longer feels accidental.

## The Invisible Pipeline

We’re told the tech industry rewards the best ideas, and everyone has an equal opportunity to succeed.

We tend to talk about tech’s gender imbalance as if it begins and ends with education: not enough girls in computer science classes, not enough women applying to engineering roles, not enough retention once they arrive. The problem is framed as a supply issue, as though the system itself is fundamentally neutral and simply waiting for more women to flow through it.

But that explanation only accounts for the visible pipeline.

There is another one operating alongside it, quieter and far more consequential—the pipeline of intellectual and institutional credibility. It’s the pathway by which someone stops being merely “a person who works in tech” and becomes something else entirely: a thought leader, a visionary, a lab director, a conference headliner, a person whose ideas are treated as shaping the future rather than simply contributing to it.

**The real pipeline in tech isn’t a university degree. It’s a dinner invitation.**

That pipeline does not run primarily through coursework or job applications. It runs through invitation-only salons like Edge, through curated stages like TED, through research institutes that decide which ideas deserve funding and which deserve to be sidelined. It runs through private dinners where introductions are made, reputations are exchanged, and legitimacy is conferred not by credentials but by social proximity.

Advancement into that world isn’t governed by a neutral process. There is no standardized test for becoming a “dangerous thinker.” There is no open application portal for intellectual authority. Instead, it depends on social trust—on who is already known, who is already vouched for, who is described by someone powerful as brilliant, original, indispensable.

Epstein understood it instinctively. He had no technical expertise to contribute, no research program of his own. What he sought was not mastery of the field but access to the machinery that certifies mastery. By embedding himself in conferences, salons, and elite research institutions, he placed himself not at the margins of tech culture but at its center—in the very spaces where credibility is manufactured and distributed.

## Culture Flows Downward

Personally, I have almost certainly never been in a room with any of these men. I can only infer from the reporting, from the emails, [from the accounts of women who have described what it felt like to move through those spaces](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-i-met-jeffrey-epstein-linda-stevens-ilvcc/). But once you recognize how many accomplished, credentialed men knew about the rape, the sex trafficking, the exploitation, and quietly accepted it, you can’t unsee it.

These spaces no longer look like a meritocracy. They look like environments governed by men who can justify or compartmentalize harm. And that matters far beyond the handful of men whose names appear in email archives.

Culture flows from the top of an industry downward. The salons, the boardrooms, the conference stages, the research institutes—these are the places where norms are set, where reputations are made, where brilliance is certified. If those spaces are overwhelmingly male, and if they have demonstrated a tolerance for overlooking harm in the name of intellectual prestige, then the signal travels outward. **It shapes what behavior is excused, who is believed, who is credible, and who is accepted.**

We often frame tech’s gender imbalance as a pipeline problem, as if the only question is how many women enter computer science programs or apply for engineering roles. But a [2024 EEOC analysis](https://web.archive.org/web/20240913233931/https://www.eeoc.gov/special-report/high-tech-low-inclusion-diversity-high-tech-workforce-and-sector-2014-2022) found that women’s representation in high-tech occupations has changed very little over nearly two decades. Despite growth, despite initiatives, despite rhetoric, the numbers have barely moved.

</MarginNote>

Perhaps that stagnation is not simply about supply. Perhaps it reflects the fact that the most powerful filter in the industry is not entry, but acceptance.

You can invite more women into a room. You can diversify a speaker lineup. But if proximity to power still depends on the endorsement of men who overlook harm, the real pipeline remains unchanged.

I used to think that we needed more women in computer science, but now I think we need fewer men who know about sexual assault and look the other way.
